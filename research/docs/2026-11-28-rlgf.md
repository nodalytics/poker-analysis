# RLGF: reinforcement learning from game feedback (design, 2026-11-28)

RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) fine-tunes a pretrained model in three steps: people compare
alternative answers, a reward model learns their preferences, and the model is trained to score well under that reward
while staying close to where it started. **RLGF keeps the three steps and replaces the people with the game.** In a
simulated hand every card is known, so the game can value every alternative action at a decision exactly as the
trainer's reward does, as often as wanted. The feedback is dense (every action of every decision, not only the hand's
result), unbiased (the reward itself, not a person's taste) and as precise as the compute spent on it.

Notation as in `2026-11-17-model-mathematics.md`: $\pi_\theta(a \mid x)$ the policy at an information state $x$ (what the
actor knows), $s$ the full game state (every card), $G$ the reward of a hand (the change in ICM prize equity, in big
blinds at the table's average chip value), $\mathcal{A}(x)$ the legal actions.

## 1. RLHF and RLGF side by side

| | RLHF | RLGF |
|---|---|---|
| Base model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ | a language model after supervised fine-tuning | a network that already plays: nm25, or the foundation model (pretrained on hands) with a policy head |
| Feedback | a person ranks two answers | the game ranks two actions: their values by rollouts on the same deals |
| Reward model | $r_\phi(x, y)$, Bradley-Terry on the rankings | $R_\phi(x, a)$, fitted to the game's action values |
| Fine-tuning | maximise $\mathbb{E}[r_\phi] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$ (PPO), or DPO on the pairs | the same objective with $R_\phi$, or DPO on the game's pairs; with a moving reference (a magnet) so that self-play converges to an equilibrium instead of a best response |
| Failure it guards against | the model drifting into text the reward model scores well but people would not | the policy exploiting its own opponents' current habits and becoming exploitable itself |

## 2. The game's feedback

### 2.1 Terminal reward (what the trainer uses today)

$$A_n = \frac{G_n - v_\theta(x_n)}{\hat\sigma}$$

One sampled outcome per decision: unbiased but noisy, because the cards still to come and every later action are luck.

### 2.2 Counterfactual action values (the new signal)

At a decision with full state $s$ and legal actions $a \in \mathcal{A}(x)$, play each action out $K$ times with the
current policies for everyone and average the reward:

$$\hat Q(s, a) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} G\big(s \xrightarrow{a} \omega_k\big)$$

where $\omega_k$ are the random draws of the rollout (the cards to come, every later action). **Common random numbers**:
the same $\omega_k$ for every action at a decision (`ninemax_search.py` already does this), so that differences are
paired:

$$\mathrm{Var}\big[\hat Q(s,a) - \hat Q(s,b)\big] = \frac{1}{K}\Big(\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_b^2 - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}_\omega\big(G_a, G_b\big)\Big)$$

which is far below $\frac{1}{K}(\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_b^2)$, because the same runout drives both. Note $\hat Q$ is conditioned
on the full state $s$ (the opponents' cards known): the feedback a perfect-information judge would give. The policy
only ever sees $x$; averaging over the states consistent with $x$ is what training does (2.4).

### 2.3 Variance reduction on the remaining luck

Two exact corrections, both leaving the expected feedback unchanged:

- **Potential-based shaping** (Ng, Harada and Russell 1999). With a potential $\Phi(s)$, the shaped reward
  $G' = G + \Phi(s_{\text{end}}) - \Phi(s_0)$ changes no policy's ranking. With $\Phi$ the all-in equity of the hands
  given the cards known so far, the river's luck is removed from every all-in.
- **Control variates on card luck** (already in the trainer: `card_luck`): subtract $c^\top L$ with $L$ the cards'
  luck per street, $\mathbb{E}[L] = 0$ by construction, and $c$ fitted by least squares (AIVAT's principle).

### 2.4 From values to feedback on the information state

The policy acts on $x$, not $s$. Sampling decisions from self-play draws $s \sim p(s \mid x)$ under the current
policies, so regressing on $x$ estimates

$$Q(x, a) = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim p(\cdot \mid x)}\big[Q(s, a)\big]$$

the value of the action against the opponents' actual ranges, exactly the quantity a policy should maximise. No belief
model is needed in training; at play time, search with beliefs (`ninemax_belief.py`) estimates the same quantity.

## 3. The game reward model

Fit $R_\phi(x, a)$ to the game's values, two ways:

**Regression** on the advantages (centred per decision, so state values cancel):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}(\phi) = \sum_n \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(x_n)} \Big(R_\phi(x_n, a) - \bar R_\phi(x_n) - \big(\hat Q(s_n, a) - \bar{\hat Q}(s_n)\big)\Big)^2$$

**Preferences** (Bradley-Terry, as RLHF): for a pair $(a, b)$ at $s_n$ the game's soft preference
$p_{ab} = \sigma\big((\hat Q(s_n,a) - \hat Q(s_n,b))/\tau\big)$ with $\tau$ the noise of the paired difference, and

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BT}}(\phi) = -\sum_{n,(a,b)} \Big[p_{ab} \log \sigma\big(R_\phi(x_n,a) - R_\phi(x_n,b)\big) + (1 - p_{ab}) \log \sigma\big(R_\phi(x_n,b) - R_\phi(x_n,a)\big)\Big]$$

The reward model generalises the expensive rollouts to every decision: rollouts label a sample (about 1% of
decisions), the model scores them all. Its quality is measured on held-out labelled decisions: the share of pairs it
orders as the game does, and the correlation of its advantages with $\hat Q$'s.

## 4. Fine-tuning the policy

### 4.1 The KL-regularised objective and its solution

$$\max_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim d^{\pi}}\Big[\sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid x)\, R_\phi(x, a)\Big] - \beta\, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim d^{\pi}}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x) \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big)\Big]$$

For each $x$ the maximiser is

$$\pi^\star(a \mid x) = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a \mid x)\, \exp\big(R_\phi(x, a)/\beta\big)}{Z(x)}, \qquad Z(x) = \sum_{a'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(a' \mid x)\, \exp\big(R_\phi(x, a')/\beta\big)$$

so the update can be taken directly, as a cross-entropy towards $\pi^\star$ on sampled decisions
($\mathcal{L} = \mathrm{KL}(\pi^\star \Vert \pi_\theta)$): no PPO clipping is needed, because the target is known in closed form
for every legal action at once (the advantage of having every action valued, which RLHF does not have).

### 4.2 DPO on the game's pairs

Equivalently, without a reward model: with $a_w, a_l$ the game's preferred and rejected action at $x$,

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\log \sigma\Big(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(a_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(a_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_l \mid x)}\Big)\Big]$$

(Rafailov et al. 2023), whose optimum is the same $\pi^\star$ when the pairs follow Bradley-Terry in $R$. Useful for the
foundation model, whose action tokens already form a distribution to compare with its own reference copy.

### 4.3 Self-play: a moving reference, towards an equilibrium

$Q$ is measured against the opponents' current play, so 4.1 with a fixed $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ converges to a best response to
them, which is exploitable. In self-play every seat is the learner, and the fix is the one the trainer already uses
(regularised Nash dynamics, R-NaD; Perolat et al. 2021): the reference becomes a **magnet** $\pi_m$ that moves slowly
towards the current policy,

$$\pi_{k+1}(a \mid x) \propto \pi_m(a \mid x)\, \exp\big(R_k(x,a)/\beta\big), \qquad \theta_m \leftarrow (1 - \tau_m)\,\theta_m + \tau_m\,\theta$$

For fixed $\pi_m$ the regularised game has a unique equilibrium the dynamics converge to; moving the magnet to it and
repeating converges to a Nash equilibrium of the game. This is the same mirror-descent step the trainer's NeuRD with a
KL to the magnet takes, with $R_k$ (the game's per-action values) in place of the single sampled advantage. Opponent
diversity (rule players, past snapshots, generated styles, the patterns people find) plays the part of many raters:
$Q$ is averaged over the opponents met.

### 4.4 Step size and noise

With $\hat Q$'s noise $\epsilon$ per action, $\pi^\star$'s log-odds move by $\epsilon/\beta$: $\beta$ sets how much noise the step
tolerates. A rule: $\beta \geq 3\,\mathrm{sd}(\hat Q(a) - \hat Q(b))$ so that a pair's order flips by noise in under 7% of
decisions; with $K$ rollouts the paired sd falls as $1/\sqrt K$, so $\beta$ can shrink as $K$ grows.

## 6. Experiments, in order

1. **Label quality.** On 400 self-play decisions, $\hat Q$ with $K$ = 16, 64, 256 against $K$ = 4,096: the paired noise
   by $K$, and so $\beta$ (4.4). Cheap.
2. **Reward model.** Fit $R_\phi$ on 1% of decisions labelled with $K$ = 64; held-out pair order agreement and
   correlation with $\hat Q$.
3. **RLGF fine-tune of nm25** (the network, before the foundation model): the 4.1 target with the magnet, labels
   refreshed every chunk, as a pre-registered pipeline run judged by the usual checks against nm25. The question: does
   per-action game feedback beat the sampled-return update at the same compute?
4. **DPO of the foundation model** on game pairs (4.2), then as a policy through the same checks.

Pass rules are written into each run file before it trains, as for every run.

## 7. Risks

- **Compute.** $K$ rollouts × $|\mathcal{A}|$ actions per labelled decision: label 1% of decisions, and let the reward
  model cover the rest.
- **Over-fitting the current opponents.** 4.3's magnet, opponent diversity, and the exploiter check guard against it.
- **A wrong reward.** The reward is the trainer's own (ICM equity change, knockouts included), so RLGF optimises the
  same thing as training does; any error there (as nm29's non-zero-sum bounty reward was) would be amplified, and the
  self-play zero-sum check stays first.
